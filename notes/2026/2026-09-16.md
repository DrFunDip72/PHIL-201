## Key Points

- Ancient ethics focused on 'living well' (eutaimonia) as a state of flourishing, distinct from modern ethics which prioritize situational moral decisions.

- Modern ethics deal with technological issues (e.g., nuclear warfare) that ancient philosophers did not face, reflecting different historical contexts and problem sets.
- Ancient Greek society had a concept of 'arete' (virtue) that was not tied to sexual ethics, allowing practices like pederasty as part of social structures.
- The lecture emphasizes the contrast between ancient and modern ethical frameworks, where ancient ethics were more community-oriented and context-specific, while modern ethics emphasize individual morality and universal values.
- The term 'eutaimonia' (Greek for 'beauty' or 'flourishing') is central to ancient ethics, representing a state of excellence in life that includes both personal and societal well-being.
- Ancient philosophy viewed the ideal ruler as a 'philosopher king' who is both a dictator and ethically perfect, contrasting with modern governance structures.
- Modern society's diversity (e.g., melting pots of cultures) makes ethical considerations more complex, requiring awareness of individual and societal impacts.
- The lecture notes that ancient ethics often had a 'procedural' approach to morality, while modern ethics focus on value judgments in specific contexts (e.g., the worth of individuals or objects).

## Summary

This study guide synthesizes key concepts from philosophical writing, logical analysis, and ethical reasoning. It emphasizes the distinction between arguments and explanations, deductive/inductive reasoning, conditional logic, and context-dependent virtues, while highlighting practical applications in academic work and real-world scenarios.

## Key Points

- Philosophy papers summarize arguments rather than extensively quote sources, prioritizing analytical depth over speculative applications.
- Deductive arguments guarantee certainty through universal principles (e.g., 'All mammals are warm-blooded'), while inductive arguments provide probabilistic conclusions (e.g., stereotypes about groups).
- Conditional statements require careful analysis of sufficient and necessary conditions (e.g., squares must have four sides), with contrapositives (e.g., 'If not Q then not P') ensuring logical validity.
- Modus ponens (if P then Q, P is true, so Q) and modus tollens (if P then Q, Q is false, so P is false) are valid argument forms but require sound premises for true conclusions.
- Virtues are context-dependent (e.g., a hammer's excellence varies by use), with Aristotle's middle ground concept between extremes (e.g., courage) and cultural influences shaping ethical definitions.
- Arguments must be evaluated stepwise for validity (premises logically lead to conclusion) before assessing soundness (true premises), avoiding fallacies like affirming the consequent.
- ESEP and IEP are common knowledge, so no citation is needed, but direct quotes require attribution; primary texts should be engaged first before secondary sources.
- Hidden assumptions in arguments must be identified, and term definitions (e.g., 'alive' vs. 'dead') impact argument validity through semantic precision.
- Epistemic humility and charity guide truth evaluation, acknowledging uncertainty in complex claims and the need to consider diverse perspectives.
- The trilemma of foundational truth verification highlights infinite regress risks in establishing absolute truths, while virtues can be intrinsic (state of mind) or extrinsic (actions).
- Big takeaway — This study guide synthesizes key concepts from philosophical writing, logical analysis, and ethical reasoning.
